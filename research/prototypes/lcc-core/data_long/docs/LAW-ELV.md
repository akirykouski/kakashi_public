# Directive 2000/53/EC on end-of-life vehicles (ELV), Article 4(2)(a)

## 1. Purpose of this compliance dossier

**1.1 Why this dossier exists**

Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. ("Valsangone") designs and produces machined housings, brake and chassis brackets, wiring-harness carriers and small die-cast assemblies for passenger-car platforms. Our plants at Rivalta, Chieri and Pinerolo, all near Turin, ship roughly 41 million parts a year to eleven vehicle assembly sites in Italy, Poland, Slovakia and Spain. None of these parts is sold to a consumer. Every one of them eventually ends up in a vehicle that will be scrapped, and the European rules on end-of-life vehicles therefore shape what we are asked to design, to declare and to document.

**1.2 What the dossier covers**

The dossier covers the following topics:

1. The purpose and use of the dossier itself (this section).
2. The background to the end-of-life vehicle rules: why they exist and how collection and treatment work across the Union.
3. Collection of vehicles and the certificate of destruction, with an explanation of the roles of vehicle makers and dismantlers and of our indirect position.
4. Further sections, held separately, that deal with specific legal provisions. One of them reproduces the legal article on hazardous substances in full. That article is deliberately kept apart from the explanatory text in this part so that the legal wording is never paraphrased or mixed with our own commentary.

The dossier is organised by topic rather than by plant. Where a plant has a local practice, the plant section says so; the general text applies everywhere.

**1.5 What the dossier is meant to achieve**

The dossier has four practical aims.

1. **Common understanding.** Each department should describe the rules in the same terms, so that answers given to customers, auditors and authorities are consistent.
2. **Clear boundaries.** The dossier states plainly which duties fall on vehicle makers, which on collection and treatment operators, and which, if any, on us. Misunderstanding here leads to two opposite mistakes: promising things we do not control, and ignoring things we do.
3. **Traceable reasoning.** When a customer asks why we keep a particular record, the answer should be found here with its background.
4. **Support for training.** The sections are written so that they can be used almost unchanged in the onboarding course for new engineers and buyers, which runs for about 90 minutes and is held four times a year.

**1.6 What the dossier does not do**

The dossier is not legal advice and does not replace the legal texts. Where the wording of a law matters, the reader must go to the law itself. It does not describe our customers' internal procedures, because those belong to the customers. It does not replace the technical specifications that accompany each part drawing. Finally, it does not create rights for third parties and is not intended for publication outside the company.

**1.7 Terms used**

To keep the text short, a few terms are used throughout:

- **End-of-life vehicle**: a vehicle that its holder discards, or intends or is required to discard, as waste.
- **Producer**: in practice the vehicle maker or the importer who places the vehicle on the market. We are a component supplier, not a producer in this sense.
- **Authorised treatment facility**: a dismantler or other operator holding a permit to treat scrapped vehicles.
- **Certificate of destruction**: the document issued when an end-of-life vehicle is handed to an authorised facility, needed to remove the vehicle from the register.
- **Dismantling information**: data that makers provide to treatment operators so that vehicles and their parts can be handled correctly.

## 2. Background: end-of-life vehicles in the EU

**2.1 The scale of the issue**

Every year a very large number of road vehicles reach the end of their useful life in the European Union. Public statistics and industry estimates put the figure at between 6 and 7 million vehicles per year, representing something like 6 to 8 million tonnes of material when an average empty mass of about 1,100 kg is assumed. In Italy alone, several hundred thousand vehicles are removed from the register for scrapping each year. For the purposes of internal planning, Valsangone uses a rounded Union-wide figure of 6.5 million vehicles a year and an average of 1,150 kg per vehicle, which gives about 7.5 million tonnes.

This stream is not simply scrap metal. Modern cars contain steel, aluminium, plastics, glass, rubber, textiles, electronics, fluids and batteries. Before the rules were introduced, many vehicles were abandoned, burned or treated in unlicensed yards where fluids drained onto the ground and the non-metallic fractions went directly to landfill. The scale of the flow made it clear that voluntary good practice would not be enough.

**2.2 Why the rules exist**

The European legislator adopted a dedicated directive for end-of-life vehicles for three connected reasons.

1. **Environmental protection.** Untreated vehicles can contaminate soil and water, and badly managed shredding creates residues that are difficult to dispose of safely.
2. **Resource efficiency.** The material in vehicles has real value. Steel and aluminium have long been recycled, but plastics, glass and technical textiles were largely lost. The rules aim to keep more of this material in use.
3. **A functioning internal market.** Without common rules, treatment standards would differ between member states, and makers would face 27 different sets of obligations. A common framework lets a vehicle be registered in one country and scrapped in another under comparable conditions.

The directive is implemented in each member state by national law. In Italy the national implementing legislation assigns tasks to the vehicle registry, to local authorities issuing treatment permits and to the environmental inspectorates.

**2.3 The waste hierarchy applied to vehicles**

The rules follow the general waste hierarchy: prevention first, then reuse, then recycling, then other recovery, and disposal only as the last option. For vehicles, prevention happens largely at the design stage. That is why makers pass requirements down to suppliers such as Valsangone: parts that are easy to separate, that are marked for material type, and that avoid problematic constructions make later treatment simpler and cheaper.

**2.4 Collection and treatment in general**

The system can be understood as a chain with five links.

1. **Holder decision.** The last owner decides to scrap the vehicle.
2. **Collection.** The vehicle is delivered, usually free of charge to the holder, to an authorised treatment facility or to a collection point linked to one.
3. **Certificate and deregistration.** The facility issues a certificate of destruction and the vehicle is removed from the register.
4. **Depollution and dismantling.** Fluids, batteries and other specified components are removed; reusable and recyclable parts are taken out.
5. **Shredding and material recovery.** The remaining hulk is shredded, metals are separated, and the non-metallic residue is further sorted or sent for energy recovery or disposal.

The principle of free take-back means that the last holder should not bear costs because the vehicle has little or no value. The cost falls on the producers in the form of take-back networks and funding arrangements, subject to national rules.

**2.5 Targets**

The directive sets minimum performance levels, calculated as an average over all vehicles treated in a member state in a calendar year and expressed by weight.

| Target | Minimum level | Calculated on |
|---|---|---|
| Reuse and recovery | 95 % | average mass per vehicle and year |
| Reuse and recycling | 85 % | average mass per vehicle and year |
| Maximum energy recovery and disposal share | 15 % | average mass per vehicle and year |

Member states report their results to the European Commission, and most have reported levels comfortably above the minimum for several years. Industry sources commonly describe the practical limit as the non-metallic shredder residue, which is the part that is hardest to recycle at economic cost.

**2.6 What a typical vehicle looks like at treatment**

The next table shows an internal planning example for a vehicle with an empty mass of 1,350 kg. It is illustrative and not a statistical result.

| Fraction | Approx. mass (kg) | Share | Typical route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ferrous metals | 810 | 60 % | Shredding, steel mill |
| Non-ferrous metals | 135 | 10 % | Separation, smelter |
| Plastics and rubber | 162 | 12 % | Dismantling, shredder residue sorting |
| Glass | 41 | 3 % | Dismantling, glass recycler |
| Fluids and batteries | 54 | 4 % | Depollution, specialist handling |
| Textiles, foam, other | 148 | 11 % | Residue sorting, energy recovery |
| **Total** | **1,350** | **100 %** | |

The example shows why metals dominate the recycling result and why the smaller fractions matter for reaching the highest targets.

**2.7 Producer responsibility and information**

The directive asks vehicle makers to design vehicles so that dismantling, reuse and recovery are facilitated, to use a rising share of recycled material where possible, and to give treatment operators information on dismantling within six months of placing a new vehicle model on the market. The information is normally made available through an international platform used by the makers. It tells operators how to find, access and remove components, where fluids are located, and which parts are designated for separate treatment.

Makers also have to coordinate a network of treatment facilities that is reasonably accessible to holders. A typical large maker in our customer base reports a network of between 300 and 1,200 contracted facilities in the countries where it sells vehicles.

**2.8 Parts marking and coding**

To help sorting, makers require standard coding of larger plastic and elastomer parts and of some components that need separate handling. Marking is carried out in the tool, not after moulding, and our tooling department maintains a library of 64 standard marking inserts for this purpose. Customer drawings state when marking is required.

**2.9 Why this matters to Valsangone**

We are not a collector, dismantler or shredder, and we hold no treatment permit. Still, the rules reach us in three ways: through contract requirements passed down by our customers, through data requests about material composition and mass, and through the technical design choices that determine how easily our parts are handled at the end of the vehicle's life. Section 3 explains the collection step in more detail and describes our indirect role.

## 3. Collection and certificates of destruction

**3.1 Purpose of this section**

This section explains what happens when a vehicle that contains our parts is handed over for scrapping, which parties carry out which tasks, and why Valsangone's role is indirect. It is meant to prevent two common misunderstandings: the belief that a component supplier issues or handles certificates, and the opposite belief that a supplier has no connection at all to what happens at the end of life.

**3.2 Who does what**

| Party | Main tasks | Documents involved |
|---|---|---|
| Last vehicle holder | Decides to scrap; delivers the vehicle or has it collected | Registration papers, identity document |
| Collection point or transporter | Receives the vehicle and passes it on without treating it, where permitted | Transfer note |
| Authorised treatment facility (dismantler) | Takes over the vehicle, checks identity, issues the certificate, depollutes, dismantles | Certificate of destruction, treatment records |
| Vehicle register authority | Removes the vehicle from the register after notification | Deregistration entry |
| Vehicle maker or importer | Funds and organises the take-back network; supplies dismantling information | Network contracts, dismantling data |
| Shredder operator | Processes the depolluted hulk and separates metals | Weighbridge and output records |
| Component supplier (Valsangone) | Designs parts and provides product data to the maker | Material data, drawings, mass declarations |

**3.3 Collection**

A holder can bring a vehicle to an authorised facility or request collection through the maker's take-back network. Italian practice allows the holder to hand the vehicle to a dealer who then passes it to a facility, but the dealer must act as an intermediary and may not store or treat the vehicle unless permitted. The take-back is free of charge for vehicles that are complete, meaning that they contain their essential components and carry no extra waste. Facilities may ask for a charge where a vehicle is missing major parts or contains added waste. Typical collection distances reported by makers in northern Italy are under 35 km for about four out of five holders.

**3.4 The certificate of destruction**

The certificate of destruction is the central document of the collection step. The authorised facility issues it when it accepts the vehicle for treatment. It confirms three facts: that the vehicle was delivered to the facility, who delivered it, and that the facility has taken responsibility for its treatment.

In practice the certificate carries:

1. the identification number of the vehicle and its registration plate;
2. the identity of the holder who delivered it;
3. the date and place of acceptance;
4. the name and permit data of the authorised facility;
5. a statement that the vehicle will be treated in line with the applicable rules.

The certificate is issued in two or more copies; one goes to the holder, and one is kept by the facility. A holder can use the certificate to show that the vehicle was properly handed over and to apply for removal from the register. In Italy the facility usually notifies the register directly, so the holder does not have to visit an office. Facilities commonly complete this notification within a few working days of acceptance.

The certificate matters beyond the register. It protects the holder from continuing tax and insurance duties for a vehicle that no longer exists as a road vehicle, and it supports the member state's statistics on how many vehicles were treated.

**3.5 Duties of the dismantler**

After issuing the certificate, the facility has to carry out treatment operations in the correct order. The first step, depollution, removes fluids and the main energy storage items. The second step removes components designated for reuse or separate recycling. The third step prepares the remaining hulk for shredding. The facility keeps records of the mass of materials leaving the site and the routes they follow, and these records are the raw data behind national reporting to the Commission. Facilities must also store vehicles on sealed surfaces with drainage and treatment of run-off, which is checked during permit inspections.

**3.6 Duties of vehicle makers**

Vehicle makers do not issue the certificate. Their duties lie around it. They set up the network of facilities that can accept their vehicles, they fund the free take-back, they supply the dismantling information, and they report on recovery and recycling performance. They also test new vehicle types before approval to show that the design allows the required levels of reuse, recycling and recovery. Makers then ask suppliers for the product data that this calculation needs.

**3.7 Our role is indirect**

Valsangone does not take part in collection, does not hold a treatment permit, and does not issue, sign or receive certificates of destruction. Our position is better described by what we contribute upstream.

1. **Product data.** We provide mass, material and composition data for each part number through the customer's supplier data system. For the 17 platforms we supply, this means data for about 2,300 active part numbers.
2. **Design for dismantling.** Where drawings require it, we use fasteners and joints that can be released with standard tools, and we provide marking on parts above the size thresholds set by the customer.
3. **Support for the maker's calculation.** Customers may ask for clarification on the recyclability of a part. The Regulatory Affairs Specialist answers within the time set in the customer request, normally 10 working days.
4. **Aftermarket parts.** Replacement parts we ship through distributors follow the same data rules as parts for new vehicles.

If a vehicle maker, dismantler or authority contacts a plant with questions about a certificate or about a specific scrapped vehicle, the plant should pass the question to the Compliance Office and should not reply directly with statements about treatment, because the plant has no knowledge of what happened to the vehicle after it left our customer's line.

**3.8 Typical timeline of a scrapped vehicle**

| Step | Typical time after the holder's decision | Responsible party |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery to facility | Day 0 to day 7 | Holder or take-back network |
| Issue of certificate | Same day as acceptance | Authorised facility |
| Notification to register | Day 1 to day 5 | Authorised facility |
| Depollution | Day 1 to day 14 | Authorised facility |
| Dismantling of reusable parts | Day 3 to day 30 | Authorised facility |
| Shredding | Day 14 to day 90 | Shredder operator |

The real timing varies by region and season. For our own planning we assume that parts we deliver today reach the dismantler after an average vehicle life of about 14 years in Italy and about 11 years in some eastern markets, which means our current design decisions affect treatment conditions in the late 2030s.

## 4. Reuse, recycling and recovery targets

## 1. Reuse, Recycling and Recovery Targets

### 1.1 Purpose and scope

This section explains how the end-of-life vehicle rules set reuse, recycling and recovery targets, how those targets reach Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. ("Valsangone") as a tier-1 supplier, and how we answer the data requests that vehicle makers send us. Valsangone does not carry the legal target itself. The target applies to the vehicle as a whole, and the vehicle maker is the party that has to show it. Our task is to give each maker accurate component-level data, so that their vehicle-level calculation rests on facts and not on estimates.

The section covers the three plants that deliver to vehicle makers: Orbassano (injection moulding and assembly of interior and under-bonnet plastic parts), Chivasso (stamped and welded steel and aluminium sub-assemblies) and Rivalta (wiring carriers, clips and small die-cast housings). It does not cover tooling, packaging or non-production material.

### 1.2 The targets in general terms

The rules express the targets as a share of the average vehicle mass, and they are set in two tiers. Each tier has two measures. One measure counts reuse and recovery together. The other counts reuse and recycling together. Recovery in this context includes energy recovery. Recycling does not.

| Measure | Earlier tier (percent of average vehicle mass) | Later tier, in force since 1 January 2015 (percent of average vehicle mass) |
|---|---|---|
| Reuse and recovery | 85 | 95 |
| Reuse and recycling | 80 | 85 |
| Share that may go to energy recovery (difference) | up to 5 | up to 10 |

Three points matter for a supplier:

1. The targets are averages over a vehicle type and are measured by mass. A heavy part that is easy to recycle counts for more than a light part that is easy to recycle.
2. Metals carry most of the result, because shredder and smelter routes recover them at very high rates. Plastics, elastomers, glass and textiles are where the margin is won or lost.
3. The maker reports the calculation as part of type approval. The approval authority checks it against a recognised calculation method that follows a standard vehicle model and a fixed treatment chain.

Valsangone's internal planning assumption, used in this dossier for sizing our own data work, is that a typical passenger car has a mass of 1,350 kg without fluids. A 95 percent recovery target therefore allows about 67.5 kg of material that is neither reused, recycled nor recovered. A 85 percent recycling target allows roughly 202.5 kg that is recovered by energy recovery or lost. These figures are illustrative only and are not a commitment to any maker.

### 1.3 How the targets reach us

The path from legal target to supplier request has four steps.

1. **Maker's target allocation.** The vehicle maker sets internal targets for each vehicle programme. These usually sit a few points above the legal minimum, to leave a margin for variation between production batches and between the vehicle types in a family.
2. **Material and mass breakdown.** The maker needs a mass by material class for every part number. Most makers take this from the central material data system described in the later IMDS section of this dossier. A smaller number ask for a separate recyclability form.
3. **Request to tier-1 suppliers.** The maker's purchasing and recycling groups send each supplier a request that names the part numbers, the format, the due date and the contact person.
4. **Our response.** Valsangone returns the data, answers queries, and takes part in the maker's recyclability review when asked.

The maker's calculation depends on the mass of each part being right to within a small margin. For that reason makers usually ask for the mass of the finished part, not the mass of the drawing's nominal volume.

### 1.4 What makers ask for

Across our eleven active vehicle programmes, the requests fall into five types. The table gives the typical content and our usual turnaround.

| Request type | Typical content | Usual format | Our internal turnaround |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recyclability questionnaire | Mass per material class, joining method, dismantling time estimate | Maker's spreadsheet or web form | 15 working days |
| Recycled content declaration | Percent of recycled material by mass, split into pre-consumer and post-consumer | Maker portal | 10 working days |
| Dismantling time estimate | Seconds to remove the part with standard tools, number of fasteners | Spreadsheet or dismantling data file | 10 working days |
| Material composition request | Full material breakdown per part number | Material data sheet in the central system | See the IMDS section |
| Target confirmation letter | Statement that supplied data are correct for named part numbers | Signed letter | 5 working days |

The recyclability questionnaire is the largest. A single questionnaire for an instrument panel carrier may list 40 to 70 lines. Our most recent questionnaire from the maker Brenta Veicoli, for a door trim module, contained 58 lines and took the Orbassano engineering team 11 working days.

### 1.5 How Valsangone prepares its answers

Each answer is built from three data sources, used in a fixed order.

1. **Bill of materials and weighed mass.** The engineering master record gives the nominal mass. For parts with a high volume, the quality laboratory also records the mean of a weighed sample of 30 pieces and the standard deviation.
2. **Material data sheets.** The material composition for each part number comes from our submitted material data. The recyclability answer must match it. If the questionnaire and the material data sheet disagree by more than 2 percent of part mass in any material class, the engineer stops and finds the cause before sending anything.
3. **Process knowledge.** Joining method (clipped, screwed, welded, bonded, overmoulded) is taken from the control plan and the assembly instruction. These decide whether the part can be separated at a dismantling site.

The following example, built with fictional numbers, shows the arithmetic for one part.

| Part: centre console carrier, Orbassano | Mass (g) | Share of part (%) | Material class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polypropylene body, glass-fibre filled | 1,240 | 82.7 | Plastic, recyclable |
| Steel reinforcement insert | 168 | 11.2 | Ferrous metal |
| Thermoplastic elastomer overmould | 52 | 3.5 | Elastomer |
| Clips and fasteners, steel | 28 | 1.9 | Ferrous metal |
| Adhesive label | 12 | 0.8 | Other |
| **Total** | **1,500** | **100.1 (rounding)** | |

The weighed mean of the 30-piece sample was 1,497 g with a standard deviation of 6 g, which is within 0.2 percent of nominal. The answer to the maker would therefore report 1,500 g.

### 1.6 Recycled content and design for recovery

Some makers now set a target for recycled content in plastic parts, separate from the end-of-life recovery targets. Valsangone's current supply position is as follows.

| Plant | Plastic parts shipped per year (tonnes) | Share made with recycled polymer, by mass (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Orbassano | 3,420 | 14 |
| Rivalta | 610 | 6 |
| Chivasso | 45 | 0 |

The weighted result is about 12.3 percent across all plants. Makers ask us whether we can raise this on specific part numbers, mainly under-body shields, wheel arch liners and hidden carriers, where appearance and surface finish are not critical. Any such offer is a commercial and technical proposal made through the normal quotation route.

For design for recovery, our engineers follow four working rules in new part designs:

1. Prefer a single polymer family within a part wherever function allows.
2. Avoid permanent bonding of unlike materials where a clip or screw would do.
3. Keep metal inserts accessible, so that a shredder or a dismantler can separate them.
4. Mark plastic parts as described in the coding section of this dossier.

### 1.7 Roles and timing

A request arriving from a maker is logged on the day it is received. The programme manager forwards it within one working day. The compliance manager assigns an owner within two working days. The owner sets an internal due date that is at least three working days before the maker's due date, so that a second person can review the figures.

### 1.8 Quality checks before sending

Before any answer leaves Valsangone, the reviewer performs these checks:

1. The part numbers in the answer match the maker's request line by line.
2. The sum of material masses equals the part mass within 1 percent.
3. The material classes follow the maker's own class list, not ours.
4. Recycled content figures are backed by supplier declarations that name the batch period.
5. The dismantling time is based on a timed trial with a trained operator, not on estimation from a similar part, unless the maker accepts an analogy in writing.
6. The reviewer's name and the date are recorded in the answer register.

In the last twelve months, the Orbassano plant answered 47 recyclability or dismantling requests. Of these, six were returned by the maker with a question about mass split, and four of those six came from joint parts where the overmould mass had been counted twice. The review check in item 2 above was tightened for this reason, and a worked example like the one in 1.5 is now attached to every internal answer file.

## 5. Coding standards and dismantling information

## 2. Coding Standards and Dismantling Information

### 2.1 Purpose and scope

The end-of-life vehicle rules expect that vehicle makers, with the help of their suppliers, make it possible for dismantlers and recyclers to identify materials and to take parts apart efficiently. Two instruments serve this goal. The first is a system of codes and marks on the parts themselves, so that a dismantler can tell what a part is made of without testing. The second is a set of dismantling information, which a maker issues to treatment operators for each vehicle model.

This section describes how Valsangone applies part marking to the plastic and elastomer parts it makes, how we meet the coding requirements of our customers, and how we contribute to the dismantling information that makers publish. It applies to the Orbassano, Rivalta and Chivasso plants. Metal parts are not marked with polymer codes, but they are covered where the maker's coding scheme asks for an alloy family or a part group code.

### 2.2 Why marking and dismantling data are needed

A dismantler has a few minutes per vehicle for each part group. A marked part can be sorted straight away to the right stream. An unmarked plastic part is often sent to mixed shredder residue, which lowers the recycling rate for the vehicle. The dismantling information tells the dismantler where each marked part sits, which tools to use, and in what order components must be removed.

For Valsangone, three points follow:

1. Marks must be present, legible and correct for the material actually used in the tool.
2. Dismantling-related data we supply to makers must match the physical part.
3. Our own tooling process must keep the mark from being lost when inserts are renewed or tools are repaired.

### 2.3 Which parts are marked

Our internal marking rule follows the common industry practice that is also applied in the standards on plastics symbols and abbreviations. A part is marked when it meets two conditions: it is made of plastic or elastomer, and its mass is above a threshold, provided there is a flat or lightly curved surface of at least 6 mm by 12 mm where the mark can be placed without affecting function or appearance.

| Part category | Mass threshold | Marking rule |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic parts, general | Above 100 g | Mark required |
| Elastomer parts | Above 200 g | Mark required |
| Plastic parts under 100 g | Not applicable | No mark unless the maker's drawing asks for one |
| Parts without a suitable surface | Any | Note in the part file; maker informed in the data answer |
| Hose and tube assemblies | Per maker coding scheme | Mark on the connector or at 150 mm intervals if the maker asks |

For the 2,184 plastic and elastomer part numbers that Valsangone supplies, 1,291 are above the threshold and have a mark on the drawing. The remaining 893 are below the threshold and are not marked. A small number, 37, are above the threshold but have no suitable surface. These are listed in the compliance register with a note for the maker.

### 2.4 How the mark is written

The mark consists of the standard abbreviation of the base polymer, placed between the symbols "greater than" and "less than". Where the polymer contains fillers or reinforcement, the filler type and content are added after the polymer code, separated by a hyphen. Where a blend is used, the codes are joined with a plus sign.

| Material | Mark on the part | Example application at Valsangone |
|---|---|---|
| Polypropylene | >PP< | Under-body shield, Orbassano |
| Polypropylene, 20 percent mineral filled | >PP-TD20< | Door trim carrier |
| Polypropylene, 30 percent glass-fibre filled | >PP-GF30< | Console carrier |
| Polyamide 6, 30 percent glass-fibre filled | >PA6-GF30< | Intake manifold bracket |
| Polyamide 66, 25 percent glass-fibre filled | >PA66-GF25< | Cable carrier, Rivalta |
| Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene and polycarbonate blend | >PC+ABS< | Instrument panel trim |
| Thermoplastic polyurethane | >TPU< | Seal lip overmould |
| Ethylene propylene diene rubber | >EPDM< | Door seal profile |

The characters are at least 3 mm high, raised or recessed by 0.2 to 0.4 mm, and set in a clean sans-serif font so that they stay readable after wear. Where an item has to meet appearance requirements, the mark is placed on the hidden side.

### 2.5 Tooling practice

The mark is made in the tool, not on the part afterwards. A mark that is added after moulding costs cycle time and is easy to omit. The Orbassano tool shop uses small replaceable inserts, so that the mark can be corrected if the polymer grade is changed or the tool is moved.

The tooling rules are:

1. Every tool drawing for a marked part shows the position, size and text of the mark.
2. The insert holding the mark is made as a separate piece, so that it can be replaced without welding the cavity.
3. At first-off tool sampling, a technician records a photograph of the mark and checks it against the material data sheet. The recorded polymer must be the same as the one in the mark.
4. When a tool is repaired, the tool shop checks that the insert is still in place and still readable.
5. Date wheels and shift marks are kept separate from material marks, so that neither can obscure the other.

In the last twelve months, our quality audits checked 214 marked parts taken at random from production. In 209 parts, the mark was legible and correct. In 5 parts, the mark was faint because the insert had worn. These findings were fed back to the tool shop as a request to shorten the inspection interval for inserts from 250,000 shots to 150,000 shots.

## 6. Material data reporting in IMDS

## 3. Material Data Reporting in IMDS

### 3.1 Purpose and scope

The international material data system, known in the industry as IMDS, is the central database through which suppliers pass the material composition of their parts to vehicle makers. Makers use these data to prepare their recovery calculations and to check their products against material rules. This section describes how Valsangone submits material data sheets, who does what, how we handle a rejection, and which deadlines we work to.

The section applies to all part numbers that Valsangone supplies to vehicle makers or to other tier-1 suppliers who pass our parts on. It does not set out customer-specific technical content, which each maker defines in its own requirements document.

### 3.2 Basic structure of a material data sheet

A material data sheet in the system is a tree. At the top is the supplied item, which is the part or assembly delivered to the customer. Below it are semi-components, basic substances and materials, each with a mass. The sheet lists every item down to the level of basic substances, and the total of the lower levels must equal the mass of the item above.

| Level | Meaning | Example at Valsangone |
|---|---|---|
| Component | Delivered part or assembly | Door trim module, 2,340 g |
| Semi-component | Sub-assembly or formed part | Moulded carrier, 1,610 g |
| Material | Mixture with a defined composition | Polypropylene, 20 percent mineral filled, 1,540 g |
| Basic substance | Individual chemical entry | Entries in the system's substance list |

The system uses a supplier identification number, called the company ID, for each legal entity. Valsangone holds one company ID for each of its three plants, which allows the customer to see the plant that produced the part.

### 3.3 Roles

The work is split between five roles. The names are fictional, and the same person may fill two roles at a small plant.

| Role | Holder | Main tasks |
|---|---|---|
| IMDS coordinator, group | Ilaria Fontanaro | Maintains the company IDs and user rights, trains new users, reviews difficult cases |
| IMDS administrator, plant | Giacomo Bellandi (Orbassano), Noemi Scaglia (Chivasso), Rocco Terzuolo (Rivalta) | Creates and sends data sheets, replies to customer comments |
| Product engineer | Per programme | Provides the bill of materials, masses and material grades |
| Purchasing buyer | Per commodity | Obtains material data sheets from sub-suppliers |
| Quality laboratory | Tomaso Reverdito | Verifies mass by weighing, checks material grade against certificate |

The IMDS administrator is the only role that sends a data sheet to a customer. The engineer prepares and the administrator enters. This division means that the engineer's technical knowledge and the administrator's system knowledge are both used, and that a second pair of eyes sees every sheet before it is sent.

### 3.4 Submission workflow

The workflow has seven steps.

1. **Request.** A customer request arrives in the system or by e-mail. It names the part numbers, the customer's recipient code and the due date. The plant administrator logs it in the data sheet tracker on the day of receipt.
2. **Bill of materials.** The product engineer issues the full bill of materials for the part, including the mass of each item, within 3 working days.
3. **Sub-supplier data.** The buyer asks each sub-supplier for its sheet. The request states the part number, the mass and the required recipient, which is Valsangone's own company ID.
4. **Entry.** The administrator builds the tree. Where a sub-supplier's sheet is available, it is linked. For materials made in-house, such as compounded blends, the administrator builds the material entry from the recipe held by the process engineer.
5. **Mass check.** The administrator compares the sum of the tree with the weighed mass from the laboratory. The permitted difference is 2 percent for parts above 100 g and 5 percent for parts below 100 g.
6. **Review.** A second administrator or the group coordinator reviews the tree against the bill of materials. The reviewer's name is recorded in the tracker.
7. **Sending.** The administrator sends the sheet to the recipient named in the request. The tracker records the date, the recipient and the sheet's internal version.

### 3.5 Deadlines

Valsangone works to internal deadlines that are shorter than those set by the maker, to allow for correction cycles.

| Stage | Maker's usual deadline | Valsangone internal target |
|---|---|---|
| First data sheet for a new part number | 10 working days before first sample delivery | 15 working days before first sample delivery |
| Data sheet for a production part number | Before the start of series production | 20 working days before the start of series production |
| Answer to a maker comment | 5 working days | 3 working days |
| Sub-supplier data request | Not applicable | Sent within 2 working days of the maker's request |
| Sub-supplier reply, expected | Not applicable | 7 working days |
| Escalation if no sub-supplier reply | Not applicable | Day 8, to the buyer's manager |

In the past year, the three plants together sent 612 data sheets. The median time from request to sending was 9 working days. The longest delay, 31 working days, was caused by a sub-supplier who could not provide a sheet for a coating system until its own raw material supplier had replied.

### 3.6 Rejections

A customer can reject a data sheet and return it with a comment. A rejection does not mean that the data are necessarily wrong. It means that the customer needs something else before it can use the sheet. The plant administrator reads each rejection on the day it arrives.

The common reasons are collected below, with the share of all rejections we received in the last twelve months. We received 74 rejections out of the 612 sheets sent, which is 12.1 percent.

| Reason for rejection | Count | Share of rejections (%) | Usual correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass of the item does not match the maker's value | 21 | 28.4 | Reweigh, correct the tree or tell the maker the reason |
| Sub-component sheet missing or wrong recipient | 16 | 21.6 | Obtain or relink the sub-supplier's sheet |
| Material classification not in the maker's list | 12 | 16.2 | Select the class the maker uses |
| Recycled content not stated where requested | 9 | 12.2 | Add declaration from supplier |
| Wrong part number or drawing index | 8 | 10.8 | Correct and resend |
| Other, including free-text comments | 8 | 10.8 | Discuss with the maker's contact |

The handling steps are as follows.

1. The administrator reads the comment and decides who needs to act: the engineer, the buyer or the laboratory.
2. The administrator starts the correction within 1 working day.
3. If the comment is unclear, the administrator calls the maker's contact. A call is preferred to an exchange of e-mails, because comments in the system are short.
4. The corrected sheet goes through the same mass check and review as the first one.
5. The administrator sends the sheet again and records the reason for the rejection in the tracker.
6. If the same reason occurs three times on the same programme in a quarter, the group coordinator opens a short review with the programme team.

A rejection is never closed by repeating the same data. The reason must be understood and the sheet must reflect the correction.

### 3.7 Data quality rules

The group coordinator has set these rules for entries made by Valsangone staff.

1. Use the standard material entries from the system's catalogue wherever a suitable one exists. Create a new material entry only when the catalogue entry does not describe the material.
2. Do not enter a mass that is a rounded figure from the drawing. Use the weighed or calculated mass.
3. Name the recipient according to the maker's list. Some makers have several plant codes, and the wrong one causes a rejection that has nothing to do with content.
4. For coatings and platings, enter the coating as a separate level with its own mass, using the supplier's data for the layer thickness.
5. Keep a copy of the supporting document, such as the material certificate or recipe, in the programme folder for the life of the programme plus 15 years.

### 3.8 Training and access

Every administrator takes the system provider's introductory course, which takes about 6 hours, and a Valsangone course of 3 hours on our own conventions. Users sign in with individual accounts and shared accounts are not allowed. The group coordinator reviews the list of active users every six months and removes people who have moved to other roles. At present, the group has 14 users with entry rights and 5 with view-only rights.

Once a year, the coordinator reviews 20 sheets chosen at random, comparing each against the bill of materials, the laboratory weight and the sub-supplier sheets. The result is discussed at the annual compliance meeting attended by the three plant managers.

## 7. Supplier declarations and our sub-supplier chain

**1.1 Purpose and scope**

This section describes how Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. collects, checks and follows up the declarations that our suppliers give us about the materials and parts they deliver. These declarations are the evidence base for our own compliance with the European rules on end-of-life vehicles. They cover all purchased parts and materials that go into our products at the plants in Orbassano, Chivasso and Pinerolo. They also cover the packaging that stays with the part until the vehicle manufacturer's line.

The section applies to production material, to service parts and to the small number of items we buy through the tooling and prototype channel. It does not cover general indirect purchases such as office supplies, cleaning services or canteen equipment, because these do not enter the product.

**1.2 Structure of the supplier base**

At the end of the last business quarter we had 412 active suppliers of production material. We group them by position in the chain and by the weight of their deliveries, because the depth of the declaration we ask for depends on both.

| Group | Description | Number of suppliers | Share of annual purchase value |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Direct suppliers of metal semi-finished goods and castings | 58 | 41% |
| B | Direct suppliers of polymer compounds, elastomers and moulded parts | 71 | 24% |
| C | Direct suppliers of electronic assemblies and wiring | 39 | 19% |
| D | Direct suppliers of coatings, adhesives, fluids and process chemicals | 47 | 6% |
| E | Distributors and traders | 64 | 7% |
| F | Fasteners, standard parts and packaging | 133 | 3% |
| Total | | 412 | 100% |

Groups A, B and C together account for 84% of purchase value and receive the full declaration package every year. Groups D and E carry a higher inherent uncertainty about material composition, so they are reviewed more often than their spend would suggest. Group F is handled mainly through catalogue-level declarations.

**1.3 Declaration forms**

We use four kinds of declaration. Each has a fixed template in our supplier portal. Suppliers can also send the content in their own format if it contains the same data fields; in that case the purchasing quality engineer maps it to our template.

Each declaration carries the name and function of the signatory, the date, the part numbers or part families covered, and a sentence confirming that the data is complete for the stated scope. A declaration without a named signatory is returned to the supplier without evaluation.

**1.4 Frequency and triggers**

There are two kinds of timing: a fixed cycle and event triggers.

*Fixed cycle.* The cycle depends on the group.

| Group | Material composition declaration | Statement of conformity | Sub-supplier disclosure |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | every 12 months | every 12 months | every 12 months |
| B | every 12 months | every 12 months | every 12 months |
| C | every 12 months | every 6 months | every 12 months |
| D | every 6 months | every 6 months | every 12 months |
| E | every 6 months | every 6 months | every 6 months |
| F | every 24 months | every 24 months | not requested |

The cycle date for each supplier is set to the anniversary of the first declaration received, so that requests are spread across the year. On average about 34 suppliers fall due in a month; the busiest month has 52 and the quietest 21.

*Event triggers.* A new declaration is requested outside the cycle when one of the following happens:

1. A supplier changes the material, the process or the production site for a part we buy.
2. A new part number is introduced into our bill of materials.
3. Incoming inspection or a laboratory screening result raises a doubt about a delivered material. Our laboratory screens roughly 180 samples per year selected by risk, using handheld spectrometry first and external laboratory analysis where needed.
4. A supplier is taken over, or its ownership changes.
5. The compliance team publishes a change in the legal framework that affects the supplier's material family (see the section on monitoring of regulatory changes).

For event triggers the supplier has 15 working days to answer.

**1.5 Receipt and checking**

Declarations arrive through the supplier portal, which sends an automatic reminder 30 days before the due date and again at 10 days. The supplier quality assistant logs each incoming declaration within 2 working days. A purchasing quality engineer then performs a four-step check:

1. Completeness: all part numbers on our current list for that supplier are covered.
2. Consistency: masses in the composition declaration add up to the part mass within a tolerance of 3%, compared against the mass held in our product data.
3. Plausibility: material classes are consistent with the drawing and the process the supplier is known to use.
4. Signature and reference: the named signatory and the legal text version are present.

Where a check fails, the engineer returns a clarification request with the specific gap. We aim for a complete check within 10 working days of receipt; the average over the last twelve months was 6.8 working days.

**1.6 Follow-up of missing declarations**

A declaration is considered missing when it has not reached us by its due date. We follow a fixed escalation ladder, and every step is recorded in the portal against the supplier.

| Step | Days after due date | Action | Responsible |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | Automatic reminder with link to template | Portal |
| 2 | 7 | Personal e-mail to the supplier's quality contact | Supplier quality assistant |
| 3 | 14 | Telephone call and request for a committed date | Purchasing quality engineer |
| 4 | 21 | Letter to the supplier's management, copy to the buyer | Supplier quality manager |
| 5 | 35 | Meeting with the supplier and the head of purchasing; sourcing review for new business | Head of purchasing |
| 6 | 60 | Decision on a supplier development plan or a source change for new part numbers | Compliance officer with head of purchasing |

We do not hold up current deliveries at steps 1 to 4. For parts in groups A to C that have no declaration at all, no new part number is awarded to the supplier until the declaration is received. In the last twelve months, 61 of 412 suppliers needed step 2 or higher, 19 needed step 4 or higher, and 4 reached step 6.

**1.7 The sub-supplier chain**

Most of the material risk lies behind our direct suppliers. We therefore ask the direct suppliers of groups A, B and C to name the sub-suppliers that contribute material or processing. Our current records show 287 named second-tier companies and 96 named third-tier companies, mostly raw material producers, compounders and plating houses in Italy, Germany, Poland, Türkiye and Asia.

We apply three rules to the chain:

1. The direct supplier remains responsible for the declaration, including the part of it that rests on sub-supplier data. We do not issue separate requests to second-tier companies unless the direct supplier fails to supply the data.
2. For the 23 part families that we regard as most sensitive to composition, we go one level deeper and ask for the second-tier declaration to be attached. These families are chosen by the compliance team each January.
3. Every year we visit or audit about 15 direct suppliers on site and ask them to show how they collect data from their own sources. The sample is selected from groups C, D and E first.

**1.8 Records and retention**

Declarations, check results and escalation records are stored in the portal and kept for the life of the part plus 15 years. Access is read-only for purchasing and engineering, and edit rights are limited to the supplier quality team and the compliance officer.

**1.9 Indicators reviewed every quarter**

## 8. Monitoring of regulatory changes

**2.1 Purpose**

The rules on end-of-life vehicles are changing in content and in detail. The main legal text is being revised at European level, annexes are adjusted from time to time, and national transposition in Italy and in the countries where we deliver does not always move in step. This section explains who at Valsangone Componenti watches for changes, how often the information is reviewed, and which sources we read. It also explains how a finding turns into internal work.

**2.2 Who watches**

Three roles share the monitoring work.

1. *Compliance officer.* Owns the monitoring process, chairs the monthly review and decides what is passed on to the technical teams. The compliance officer devotes about 20% of working time to monitoring.
2. *Regulatory analyst.* A full-time position in the quality department. Reads the sources daily, keeps the watch list and writes the monthly summary. The analyst has a deputy in the laboratory team who covers holidays and absences, so that no period of more than five working days passes without a person reading the sources.
3. *Technical correspondents.* Five engineers, one each from product engineering, materials engineering, purchasing quality, logistics and the legal department. Each reads the sources relevant to their own field and reports anything the analyst may have missed.

In addition, our external legal counsel in Turin provides a written note twice a year and answers specific questions within 5 working days. We pay a fixed annual fee for this service, and extra questions are charged by hours.

**2.3 Sources we read**

We distinguish primary sources, which are the legal texts themselves, from secondary sources, which interpret them. The analyst always goes back to the primary source before a finding is passed on.

| Type | Source | How we use it | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Official Journal of the European Union, legislation series | Alerts for new acts, corrections and annex changes in the vehicle sector | Daily |
| Primary | European Commission pages on the vehicle recycling framework, including delegated acts and committee documents | Early view of proposals and timelines | Weekly |
| Primary | Italian Gazzetta Ufficiale and ministry publications | National transposition and implementing decrees | Daily |
| Primary | Official publications of the other countries where we deliver (Germany, France, Poland, Spain) | Transposition differences | Weekly |
| Secondary | Association of Italian automotive component makers, technical circulars | Interpretation and industry position | On publication |
| Secondary | European automotive suppliers association, regulatory newsletter | Cross-country view, consultation deadlines | Weekly |
| Secondary | Law firm bulletins (three subscriptions) | Commentary on draft texts | On publication |
| Secondary | Industry material data platform user group notes | Changes in data formats and reporting rules | Monthly |
| Secondary | Standards body notices on relevant test and sampling methods | Method changes that affect laboratory work | Monthly |

The analyst uses an alert service with 14 saved search profiles. Over the last twelve months the alerts produced about 1,150 hits, of which 212 were read in full, 37 were entered on the watch list and 9 led to technical work.

**2.4 The watch list**

Every item that may become relevant goes on the watch list. Each entry has the following fields:

1. Short title and the reference of the legal text or proposal.
2. Stage in the legislative path (for example proposal, position of the institutions, adoption, entry into force, application date).
3. Dates that matter to us: consultation deadlines, entry into force and application date.
4. Affected product families and supplier groups, using the group letters from our supplier base.
5. Name of the technical correspondent who follows the item.
6. Initial assessment of impact on a scale of low, medium and high, and a short reason.

The list currently holds 31 entries. Of these, 6 are rated high impact, 14 medium and 11 low. The analyst reviews the stage and dates of each entry at least once a month, and weekly for entries with a date inside the next 90 days.

**2.5 Monthly review**

The monthly review takes place on the second Thursday of each month and lasts 90 minutes. Participants are the compliance officer, the regulatory analyst, the five technical correspondents and, as needed, the head of purchasing or the plant managers.

The agenda is fixed:

| Item | Time | Led by | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| New findings since last meeting | 20 min | Regulatory analyst | Decision to add to the watch list or discard |
| Change in stage or dates of existing entries | 15 min | Regulatory analyst | Updated dates |
| Impact discussion for high and medium entries | 30 min | Technical correspondents | Impact rating confirmed or changed |
| Actions for technical teams and purchasing | 15 min | Compliance officer | Action list with owners and due dates |
| Training and communication needs | 10 min | Compliance officer | Input to the training plan |

The analyst circulates a written summary within 3 working days. The summary lists decisions, actions with names and dates, and a one-paragraph statement for each high-impact entry that can be forwarded to the plants. An average of 4.2 actions came out of each meeting during the last twelve months.

**2.6 From finding to internal work**

When the monthly review decides that a change needs a reaction, the compliance officer assigns the work in one of four directions:

1. *Supplier side.* Purchasing quality prepares a request to the affected supplier groups, adjusts templates or changes the cycle in the supplier declarations (see the section on supplier declarations).
2. *Product side.* Engineering reviews drawings, material specifications and the bill of materials for affected product families.
3. *Laboratory side.* The laboratory checks whether screening methods and limits of detection remain suitable.
4. *Training side.* The compliance officer adds the topic to the next training round.

For each direction the owner proposes a date, which the compliance officer confirms at the next monthly meeting or earlier by e-mail. We aim to start work on any high-impact item within 10 working days of the monthly review, and the compliance officer reports progress to the quarterly compliance review.

**2.7 Consultations and association work**

We take part in public consultations where our products are directly affected. In the last twelve months we gave input through the Italian association to 3 consultations and responded directly to 1. The regulatory analyst drafts the technical content, the legal department checks it, and the compliance officer signs. Two staff members attend the association's working group on vehicle recycling, which meets about six times a year.

**2.8 Quality of the monitoring**

Once a year, in the autumn, the compliance officer carries out a check on the monitoring itself. The analyst picks 10 legal acts or proposals from the previous twelve months, selected by the compliance officer from the official journal without consulting the watch list. The team then checks whether each one was seen, when, and by which source. The target is that 100% of the acts that apply to our sector were seen at least 30 days before their entry into force, and that no act was found first through a customer or supplier.

## 10. Internal audit of compliance

**1.1 Purpose and scope**

This section describes how Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. checks, from the inside, that its parts and processes meet the EU end-of-life vehicles rules as they apply to a tier-1 supplier. The audit covers the three production sites (Rivalta, Chivasso and Orbassano), the central engineering office in Turin, and the purchasing group that manages 186 active tier-2 suppliers. It covers material declarations, supplier evidence, laboratory results, marking and dismantling information, and the competence of the people who handle these items.

The audit tests whether the compliance system works in practice. It does not replace the daily checks done by engineering and quality staff. Auditors must not audit work they carried out or supervised in the previous 12 months.

**1.2 Audit programme and cadence**

The Compliance Manager prepares the annual programme in November for the following calendar year. The programme is built on risk: a site or part family with a higher volume, a newer design or a weaker supplier history is audited more often.

| Audit type | Frequency | Typical duration | Team | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full system audit, each site | Once per year per site | 3 days | 2 auditors + 1 observer | Audit report |
| Part family deep dive | 6 per year across all sites | 1 day | 1 auditor + 1 engineer | Short report |
| Supplier evidence audit (remote) | 40 suppliers per year | 2 hours each | 1 auditor | Supplier evidence note |
| Follow-up audit after a major finding | Within 60 days of the finding | 0.5 to 1 day | 1 auditor | Verification note |
| Unannounced spot check | 4 per year per site | 2 hours | 1 auditor | Check sheet |

The company keeps a pool of 9 trained internal auditors. Each one completes a 2-day course and at least 3 audits as a trainee before leading an audit alone. Refresher training takes place every 24 months.

**1.3 Audit checklist**

The checklist has 48 questions in 8 groups. Auditors use the same checklist at every site so that results can be compared. Each question is scored as conforming, minor gap, major gap or not applicable, and the auditor must write down the evidence examined.

| Group | Number of questions | Example question | Evidence examined |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Governance | 5 | Is a named owner assigned to each part family? | Responsibility matrix, organisation chart |
| B. Material declarations | 9 | Does each declaration match the current bill of materials? | Declaration file, bill of materials extract |
| C. Supplier evidence | 8 | Does each supplier statement cover the delivered part number and version? | Supplier statements, purchase orders |
| D. Laboratory and screening | 7 | Are screening instruments calibrated and are results traceable to a sample? | Calibration certificates, test logs |
| E. Marking and dismantling information | 6 | Do component markings on 5 production parts match the drawing? | Drawings, physical parts |
| F. Change handling inside the company | 5 | Was the compliance review step performed before release to production? | Engineering change records |
| G. Training and competence | 4 | Have the 12 sampled employees completed the annual course? | Training matrix, attendance sheets |
| H. Records | 4 | Can the record be found within 2 working days? | Filing index, retrieval test |

**1.4 Sampling of parts**

The company has 412 active finished part numbers, which are grouped into 14 part families. Auditors use stratified sampling so that every family is looked at at least once in each 12-month period.

The rules are as follows:

1. Each site audit takes a minimum of 20 part numbers, or 10 percent of the active part numbers produced at that site, whichever is larger.
2. At least one part number per family produced at the site is included.
3. At least 30 percent of the sample is chosen by the auditor using a random number list. The rest is chosen on risk grounds, for example parts with a new tier-2 supplier, parts with a design change in the last 6 months, or parts with a past finding.
4. For each selected part number, the auditor takes the declaration file and traces one component back to the supplier statement and, where available, to a laboratory result.

| Site | Active part numbers | Part families | Minimum sample | Planned sample this year | Physical parts inspected |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rivalta | 168 | 6 | 20 | 24 | 8 |
| Chivasso | 131 | 5 | 20 | 20 | 6 |
| Orbassano | 113 | 5 | 20 | 20 | 6 |
| Total | 412 | 14 (some shared) | 60 | 64 | 20 |

Because some families are produced at more than one site, the family count at the site level adds up to more than 14.

**1.5 Depth of testing for each sampled part**

For each sampled part number the auditor completes five steps:

1. Compare the declared composition with the current bill of materials and mark any line that differs.
2. Select 3 components or materials from the bill of materials and trace each one to the supplier statement.
3. Check that the supplier statement names the same part number, version and delivery site as the purchase order.
4. Check whether a laboratory or screening result exists for the traced item and whether its sample identification links to a production lot.
5. For the 20 physical parts, check markings, material codes and the data given to dismantlers against the drawing.

If the tracing in step 2 reveals one gap, the auditor expands the sample for that family by 5 additional part numbers. If the expanded sample reveals a second gap, the auditor classifies the issue as systemic and reports it as such.

Sample size for the 40 remote supplier audits is set separately. Auditors choose suppliers by spend and risk, so that the 40 suppliers together cover at least 70 percent of annual purchase value for materials in scope.

**1.6 Classification of findings**

Every gap found is written as a finding with a short factual statement, the checklist question it relates to, and the evidence. The auditor assigns one of three classes.

| Class | Definition | Example | Containment deadline | Corrective action plan due | Effectiveness check |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Major | The system fails to meet a rule, or a gap affects parts already delivered | A supplier statement is missing for a delivered component | 2 working days | 10 working days | Within 60 days |
| Minor | An isolated lapse that does not affect delivered parts | A declaration shows an outdated version letter | 10 working days | 30 working days | At next audit |
| Observation | A weakness that could become a gap | A filing index is hard to search | Not required | Optional | At next audit |

## 11. Records and evidence

**2.1 Principle**

Compliance with the end-of-life vehicles rules can only be shown if evidence can be found, read and trusted years after a part was designed. Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. therefore treats compliance records as controlled records. Each record has an owner, a filing location, a retention period and a method of disposal. This section defines these four elements. The audit method for checking them is described in the internal audit section of this dossier.

**2.2 Evidence pack for each part number**

For every finished part number the company keeps one evidence pack. The pack is a folder in the compliance repository and holds the following items:

1. The material declaration for the part and its current bill of materials extract.
2. Supplier statements for each declared component or material.
3. Laboratory reports and screening logs linked to the part or its materials.
4. Marking and dismantling information, including the drawing extract that shows material codes.
5. The record of the internal compliance review performed before production release.
6. Correspondence that explains a decision, such as an engineering query to a supplier and the reply.

A pack is considered complete when it has all six item types or a written note explaining why one type does not apply. The Compliance Manager checks 15 packs each quarter for completeness as part of the records group of the audit checklist.

**2.3 Where evidence is filed**

The company uses four filing locations. Each one has a defined purpose, so staff do not need to guess where a document belongs.

| Location | Content | Format | Owner | Access |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Compliance repository (central server in Turin, mirrored to a second data centre in Milan) | Evidence packs, audit reports, training records | Electronic, searchable PDF and native files | Compliance Manager | Read: 64 named users; write: 11 named users |
| Product data system | Bills of materials, drawings, engineering change records | Electronic, native | Engineering Director | Read: all engineering; write: release engineers |
| Laboratory folder (Rivalta quality laboratory) | Raw instrument output, sample logs, calibration certificates | Electronic and paper logbooks | Laboratory Supervisor | Read: quality staff; write: 5 laboratory technicians |
| Site archive rooms (one per site) | Signed paper originals, supplier letters received on paper | Paper, in labelled boxes | Site Quality Manager | Key held by 3 people per site |

When a record exists in both paper and electronic form, the electronic scan is the working copy and the paper is the original. The scan must be filed within 5 working days of receipt and must be checked for legibility by a second person.

**2.4 Naming and indexing**

Files are named using a fixed pattern: site code, part family code, part number, record type and date in year-month-day order. Free text is not allowed in the file name. A master index lists every pack with its location, owner and retention end date. The index holds about 9,500 entries and is checked each year against the contents of the repository. Any entry without a file, or any file without an entry, is treated as a finding by the audit team.

Paper boxes carry a label with the same pattern, a box number unique within the site, and the destruction date. Chivasso uses 310 boxes, Orbassano 275 and Rivalta 420.

**2.5 Retention periods**

Retention is counted from a trigger event, not from the date the record was created. For compliance records on delivered parts the trigger is the last delivery of the part number to any customer. The periods below were set by the Legal Department on the basis of the expected service life of vehicles and the company's limitation period for claims.

| Record type | Trigger event | Retention period | Disposal method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material declarations and evidence packs | Last delivery of the part number | 15 years | Electronic deletion, logged |
| Supplier statements | Last delivery of the supplied item | 15 years | Electronic deletion, logged |
| Laboratory reports and screening logs | Date of the report | 12 years | Electronic deletion; paper shredded |
| Raw instrument output | Date of measurement | 5 years | Electronic deletion |
| Calibration certificates | Date of certificate | 6 years | Shredding |
| Internal audit reports and checklists | Date of audit | 10 years | Electronic deletion; paper shredded |
| Finding logs and corrective action records | Date of the last action | 10 years | Electronic deletion |
| Training records and attendance sheets | End of employment | 6 years | Shredding |
| Engineering change records | Last delivery of the affected part | 15 years | Electronic deletion |

When two periods apply to one record, the longer period governs. A record is never kept for a shorter time because the file is large or the storage is expensive.

**2.6 Legal hold**

The Legal Department can place a hold on any set of records when a dispute, an authority request or an internal investigation is expected. A held record is flagged in the master index, blocked from deletion, and moved to a protected folder where only 3 named people can open it. Disposal resumes only after Legal confirms in writing that the hold has ended. The Compliance Manager receives a list of all active holds each January.

**2.7 Integrity, backup and access**

The following controls apply to the compliance repository:

1. Daily incremental backup and weekly full backup, with 35 days of backup history.
2. Version history is kept for every file, and the last 20 versions can be recovered.
3. Write access is reviewed every 6 months. Staff who change role lose access on the day of the change.
4. Files for completed audits are stored read-only. A correction is added as a new file next to the original and never replaces it.
5. Paper archive rooms are fitted with fire detection and kept between 15 and 25 degrees Celsius.

**2.8 Retrieval test**

The company's target is to produce any compliance record within 2 working days of a request. To check this, each quarter the audit team selects 12 records at random from the master index, of which at least 4 are older than 8 years. The team measures the time to retrieve each one and checks that it is readable and complete. The target for the quarter is 12 out of 12 retrieved on time. Last year's results were 47 of 48 on time, and the single late record was a paper original that had been placed in the wrong box.

**2.9 Disposal**

Disposal is carried out once per year, in March, for all records whose retention end date passed in the previous calendar year. The Site Quality Manager and one witness from another department sign a disposal list. Paper is shredded by a certified contractor and a certificate of destruction is filed. Electronic deletion is carried out by the IT Service Desk and logged with the number of files removed. The disposal list itself is kept for 10 years.

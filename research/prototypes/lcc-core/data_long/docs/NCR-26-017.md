# NCR-26-017 CL-115 hardness out of tolerance

## 1. D0 Symptom and emergency response

### 0.1 Summary of the symptom

CL-115 is a stamped and hardened cable clip made from 1.2 mm C67S spring steel strip. Valsangone Componenti S.p.A. buys it from Minuterie Germanasca. At the Valsangone plant, a polymer sleeve is pressed onto each clip in the sleeve-insertion cell, and the clip is then packed into kits of 200 pieces. The drawing requires a hardness of 410 to 450 HV1 after tempering. This corresponds to a clip that bends elastically through its full snap-in travel and returns to shape.

On Tuesday 3 November 2026, operators in the sleeve-insertion cell found that clips were cracking at the bend radius of the retaining tab. This happened when the tab was pressed over the sleeve shoulder. Cracking was audible and visible, and it was not seen on earlier shifts. A bench check that morning measured clips between 485 and 540 HV1. This is 35 to 90 HV1 above the upper limit. A clip this hard has low ductility and may fracture during installation or later in service. For that reason the finding was treated as a risk to the function of delivered parts, not only as a production loss.

### 0.5 Protective actions in the first shift

#### 0.5.1 Stopping the process

Cell 3 stayed stopped until the laboratory had measured the clips. The two other cells that press sleeves onto CL-115 were checked by their supervisors at 08:20. Cell 1 had last run CL-115 on 29 October, and cell 2 was running a different clip. Only cell 3 held clips from the fourth delivery.

#### 0.5.2 Protecting people

A fractured clip can have a sharp edge, and a clip that breaks under tension can fly off. Ferrando told the cell operators to wear safety glasses and cut-resistant gloves while pressing clips, until the cell was stopped. Zanetti noticed no injuries. The cracked clips were collected with a magnet, not by hand.

#### 0.5.3 Protecting stock

The warehouse freeze blocked the picking of CL-115 in the ERP system, so a clip could not be sent to a line by mistake. The physical red tags on every pallet, bin and kit carton were added because the ERP block does not stop a person who takes parts by hand. Oldani made one walk through the warehouse at 10:00 and one through the hall at 10:40, and found no tags missing.

#### 0.5.4 Protecting the delivered quantity

The first concern was that kits might already have reached the customer. At T+30 hours, Oldani's list showed that 46,000 clips, in five shipments, had left Valsangone since 12 October. The customer quality liaison received the dates and kit quantities of the five shipments on the afternoon of 4 November, so that the customer could check its own receiving stock while Valsangone began its sorting.

### 0.6 Early data

The retained samples gave the following picture. Hardness was measured with a micro-hardness tester at 1 kgf on a ground cross-section at mid-leg.

| Delivery | Date received | Quantity | Clips measured | Mean HV1 | Min HV1 | Max HV1 | Clips out of 410 to 450 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 12 October 2026 | 48,000 | 30 | 438 | 421 | 452 | 2 of 30 |

| 3 | 26 October 2026 | 60,000 | 30 | 493 | 468 | 524 | 30 of 30 |

The hardness rises across the four deliveries. This points to a drift over time at the supplier, and not to a single bad lot. At this point the cause is unknown. A tempering temperature that was too low, a belt speed that was too high, or a change of strip material are all possible. D0 does not choose between them, and the supplier was asked for its records.

The data also shows that the whole quantity could not be released, because even the first two deliveries contained out-of-limit clips. Sampling of 30 pieces cannot show the actual share of bad clips in a delivery of 48,000. For this reason the team decided to sort every piece, and to design the sorting so that it can handle large quantities (see D3).

### 0.7 Open information needs at the end of the emergency phase

- The supplier's tempering furnace records, with temperatures and belt speed, for all four delivery dates.
- The strip coil certificates for the material used in each delivery.
- A reading of the hardness of the supplier's own retained samples.
- A decision on how many clips the plant needs in the next two weeks, so that production planning can use sorted stock or a replacement supply.

These needs were handed to the team formed under D1.

### 3.3 Sorting plan

#### 3.3.1 Choice of method

A hardness measurement on each clip with an indenter would leave a mark and would take too long. The team chose a non-destructive eddy-current comparator that Rinaldi's group already uses for other spring parts. The device compares the electromagnetic response of each clip with that of reference clips. A harder clip gives a different signal from a softer one, so the device can separate the two.

The method was not used before on CL-115, so it was first proven. Cossu prepared 40 reference clips from the retained samples, each measured at HV1 on a section and then marked on the clip. He sorted them as follows.

| Reference group | Number of clips | Hardness range, HV1 | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: in limit, centre | 12 | 425 to 445 | Set the accept signal |
| B: in limit, near the upper edge | 8 | 446 to 450 | Set the accept limit |
| C: just above the limit | 10 | 452 to 470 | Test for the smallest detectable step |
| D: well above the limit | 10 | 485 to 540 | Set the reject signal |

The comparator limit was set at the signal level between groups B and C. The device then sorted all 40 reference clips correctly in three repeated runs. As the device had no error in these runs, the team added a safety margin: any clip within 5 per cent of the limit signal is also rejected. This gives up some good clips, and it reduces the risk that a bad clip is accepted.

#### 3.3.2 Checks during sorting

The comparator is checked with groups A and D at the start of every shift and after every 5,000 clips. If it misreads a reference clip, all clips sorted since the last good check are sorted again. Each shift also takes a sample of 50 accepted clips for a hardness measurement on sections by Cossu's laboratory. The sorting stops if one clip in a sample of 50 is above 450 HV1.

#### 3.3.3 Sorting steps by form

| Form | Method | Throughput | Staff | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loose clips | Vibratory feeder into the comparator, automatic air-jet separation | about 2,400 clips per hour | 2 inspectors | Accept, reject and doubtful clips go to three bins |
| Clips with sleeve | Comparator with a larger probe, manual load | about 900 clips per hour | 3 inspectors | The polymer sleeve does not affect the signal, which was checked with 20 reference clips |
| Kits of 200 | Kits opened, clips and sleeves sorted separately, then repacked | about 600 clips per hour | 4 operators | Good sleeves are kept for reuse |

The doubtful bin takes all clips for which the signal is close to the limit. They are measured again, and if the second reading is also doubtful, they are treated as rejected.

## 4. Measurement results

### 1.1 Purpose and scope

The measurements cover six strip lots received between July and November 2026. They also cover the finished clips made from those lots and the checks carried out to confirm the measuring equipment was reliable.

### 1.2 Acceptance criteria used

The drawing requires a finished-clip hardness of 430 to 480 HV1, measured on a polished cross-section at mid-thickness. The material specification for the strip requires a tensile strength of 1,400 to 1,600 MPa. The strip thickness tolerance is 0.80 mm ± 0.03 mm. Where a measurement in this section is called out of range, it is measured against these limits only.

### 1.3 Sampling plan

Samples were drawn per lot, from the head and tail of each coil and from the middle of the coil where the coil length allowed. For finished clips, samples were taken from the first, middle and last thirds of each production run. The sample sizes are shown in the results tables. Samples were mounted in a cold-setting resin, ground and polished before any hardness indentation, so that surface cold work from stamping could not influence the readings.

## 5. D4 Ishikawa analysis

### 2.1 Approach

The team built a cause-and-effect diagram around the problem statement: "CL-115 clips made from Germanasca C67S strip measure 487–538 HV1 against a limit of 430–480 HV1 and crack during installation." The team consisted of the quality engineer, the stamping process engineer, the incoming inspection lead, the laboratory technician and a purchasing representative. A metallurgist from the external laboratory and the Germanasca quality manager joined by video for the material and measurement branches. Each of the six branches below was assessed against the evidence in the measurement results. A cause was kept for further testing only when it could plausibly raise hardness uniformly across three consecutive lots.

**Table 5 – Summary of Ishikawa branches**

| Branch | Candidate causes raised | Carried to verification | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Man | 4 | 1 | Inspection reliance on certificate |
| Machine | 6 | 2 | Supplier tempering furnace control |
| Method | 5 | 2 | Supplier line speed and Valsangone acceptance method |
| Material | 5 | 1 | Carbon and alloy content check |
| Measurement | 5 | 1 | Supplier single-reading hardness check |
| Environment | 3 | 0 | No link to hardness |

### 2.2 Man

Four candidate causes were reviewed.

1. **Incoming inspector skipped a hardness check.** The inspection record shows that incoming inspection on strip covers thickness and width at each coil and does not include hardness. The inspector followed the instruction as written. This is not an individual error but it is a gap in what is asked of the person, and it explains why the problem reached the stamping line.
2. **Stamping operator mixed lots.** Lot traceability on the press shows no mixing. Lots 3, 4 and 5 were run in separate shifts with clear label records.
3. **Laboratory technician reading error.** Two technicians measured the same 10 mounts independently, and the results differed by a mean of 1.8 HV. This cannot account for a 45 HV shift.
4. **Supplier operator adjusted furnace settings.** The supplier operator log shows a line-speed increase on 15 September. This is carried to the Method branch.

The man branch therefore supports one point only: the people followed the written instructions, and the instructions did not ask for a hardness check on arrival.

### 2.3 Machine

Six causes were raised, covering both sites.

1. **Valsangone press or tooling work-hardening the strip.** Strip and clip hardness agree within 1.5 HV (Table 3), so tooling does not add hardness. Ruled out.
2. **Valsangone stamping force or speed.** Press settings were unchanged across all runs. Ruled out.
3. **Supplier tempering furnace temperature below setpoint.** The supplier's continuous tempering furnace has three zones controlled by one thermocouple each. Germanasca reported that the thermocouple in zone 3 was replaced on 10 September and checked against a handheld reference on 25 September, when it read 28 °C higher than the reference. A reading that is too high makes the controller supply less heat, so the strip is tempered cooler than intended. Carried to verification.
4. **Supplier quench cooling rate too high.** A faster quench would raise as-quenched hardness but tempering normally brings strip to a similar value. The supplier's quench data was unchanged. Considered unlikely.
5. **Supplier strip tension or leveller settings.** These affect flatness and not hardness. Ruled out.
6. **Supplier austenitising zone temperature.** Logged values stayed within ±5 °C. Carried to verification as a secondary check, together with the temper-time issue under Method.

### 2.4 Method

Five causes were reviewed.

1. **Supplier line speed increased.** The supplier raised line speed from 5.0 to 5.6 m/min on 15 September to meet a volume request. Soak time in the tempering zone fell from about 95 s to 85 s. Carried to verification.
2. **Supplier austenitising soak reduced.** The same speed increase reduced austenitising time. This affects grain size and carbide dissolution more than final hardness. Carried to verification through metallography.
3. **Valsangone acceptance method relies on the certificate.** The purchasing specification calls for a certificate with each lot and does not request a hardness check on arrival. This explains why the defect escaped, and it is carried forward with the Man branch.
4. **Valsangone storage method alters the strip.** Strip is stored at ambient temperature for less than 60 days. A temperature rise of this kind cannot change hardness. Ruled out.
5. **Clip forming sequence causes strain hardening.** Ruled out by Table 3.

### 2.5 Material

Five causes were reviewed.

1. **Carbon content above specification.** Higher carbon would raise the hardness after the same heat treatment. The specification range is 0.65 to 0.70 percent carbon. Spectrometer results for the three affected lots are reported in Section 3. Carried to verification.
2. **Alloy content such as manganese or silicon outside range.** Checked in the same spectrometer run.
3. **Wrong strip grade delivered.** The mill certificate and the spectrometer both show the correct grade family. Ruled out unless the spectrometer result contradicts it.
4. **Different steel mill source.** Germanasca confirmed that Lots 3 to 5 came from the same mill as Lots 1 and 2.
5. **Strip decarburised on the surface, lowering instead of raising hardness.** The direction is wrong for this defect. Ruled out.

### 2.6 Measurement

Five causes were reviewed.

1. **Valsangone tester reading high.** The reference block checks (Table 1) are within 1.2 HV, and the external laboratory's tester B gave results within 3.1 HV of tester A on 12 shared mounts. Ruled out.
2. **Specimen preparation raising hardness.** Mounts were cold-set and polished with light pressure. Surface layers were removed by at least 0.05 mm. Ruled out.
3. **Indentation too close to the edge.** Spacing was held above 2.5 diagonals from any edge. Ruled out.
4. **Conversion error between scales.** Hardness was read directly in HV, so no conversion was involved. Ruled out.
5. **Supplier measurement system.** The supplier certificate is based on one reading per coil, taken at the coil head with a portable tester. A single reading cannot show a drift of this size reliably, and the portable tester was last verified 14 months earlier. Carried to verification as a contributor to the escape.

### 2.7 Environment

Three causes were reviewed.

1. **Temperature of the laboratory.** The room was held between 20 °C and 23 °C. Hardness testers are insensitive to this range.
2. **Vibration near the tester.** The tester stands on an isolated bench. Reference blocks gave stable readings.
3. **Humidity or storage conditions at Valsangone.** Humidity affects surface rust, not through-thickness hardness. Ruled out.

The team found no environmental cause.

### 2.8 Conclusion of the Ishikawa review

The diagram points toward two production-side causes at the supplier, a tempering temperature below the intended value and a shorter soak time after a line-speed increase, and two detection-side causes, a one-reading supplier check and the lack of a hardness check at Valsangone incoming inspection. Chemistry stayed on the list until it could be checked in the laboratory. The remaining causes were set aside with evidence. Section 3 tests the retained causes.

## 6. D4 Five-why analysis and verification tests

### 3.1 Approach

The five-why analysis follows two chains. The first explains why the strip left the supplier too hard. The second explains why it was not detected before stamping. The laboratory tests that support each answer are described afterwards, so every answer in a chain can be traced to a measurement.

### 3.2 Why chain A – occurrence

**Problem:** Strip in Lots 3, 4 and 5 is 20 to 55 HV1 above the upper limit.

1. **Why was the strip too hard?** Because the martensite formed on quenching was tempered less than intended. The microstructure and re-temper tests in 3.6 and 3.7 show this.
2. **Why was it tempered less than intended?** Because the effective strip temperature in the tempering furnace was lower than the 420 °C setpoint, and the soak time was shorter than the validated 95 s.
3. **Why was the temperature lower and the time shorter?** The zone 3 thermocouple read 28 °C high after replacement, so the controller under-heated, and line speed was raised by 12 percent on 15 September.
4. **Why was the thermocouple error not detected, and why was line speed changed?** The replacement thermocouple was installed without a check against a reference, and the line-speed change was made as a scheduling decision without a heat-treatment review.
5. **Why was there no check or review?** The supplier's furnace procedure requires a reference check at the next monthly audit and not at replacement, and it does not list line speed as a controlled parameter for the tempering step.

**Root cause A:** The supplier's furnace control procedure allowed a thermocouple replacement and a line-speed increase without verifying the resulting tempering conditions.

### 3.3 Why chain B – escape

**Problem:** Out-of-range strip reached the stamping line and was formed into clips.

1. **Why did hard strip reach the stamping line?** Because incoming inspection did not measure hardness.
2. **Why did incoming inspection not measure hardness?** The inspection instruction for the strip lists thickness, width and surface only.
3. **Why does the instruction omit hardness?** When the material was first approved, the supplier had a clean record over 30 consecutive lots, and hardness was left to the certificate.
4. **Why was the certificate not questioned?** It showed values within range for every lot, and there was no comparison between certificate and plant data.
5. **Why was there no comparison?** The purchasing specification treated the certificate as sufficient evidence and had no periodic verification built in.

**Root cause B:** Valsangone's incoming inspection relied on a supplier certificate based on one reading per coil, with no independent hardness check.

### 3.4 Chemical composition check

The team had retained chemistry as a candidate. Samples from Lots 2, 3, 4 and 6 were analysed by optical emission spectrometry at the external laboratory, with three burns per sample.

### 3.6 Furnace data review at the supplier

Germanasca supplied furnace logs for the production dates of Lots 2 to 6. For Lot 2, zone 3 showed an indicated 420 °C and a handheld reference of 419 °C. For Lots 3 to 5, zone 3 indicated 420 °C while the reference showed 392 °C. The shortfall of 28 °C matches the offset found on 25 September. With the shorter soak time, the supplier's heat-treatment engineer estimated the effective tempering condition to be equivalent to roughly 360 to 370 °C for 95 s. For Lot 6, the thermocouple had been replaced again and the reference read 418 °C.

### 3.7 Re-temper response test

To test whether the hard strip was simply under-tempered, coupons from Lot 2 and Lot 4 were re-tempered for 30 minutes in a laboratory furnace at several temperatures, then measured on Vickers tester A (n = 5 per condition).

### 3.8 Tensile and bend confirmation

Coupons re-tempered at 420 °C from Lot 4 gave a mean tensile strength of 1,578 MPa, inside the limit. Clips formed from re-tempered strip passed the 50-piece bend test with zero cracks in three repeats of 50, compared with 7 cracks in 50 for as-received Lot 4 clips.

### 3.9 Confirmation of root cause

The evidence from each test points in the same direction:

1. Chemistry is identical across conforming and nonconforming lots.
2. Grain size is identical, and carbides are finer in the hard lots.
3. The furnace reference check shows a 28 °C shortfall and a shorter soak time for the affected period.
4. Re-tempering hard strip returns it to the expected hardness and restores bend performance.
5. Lot 6, made after the thermocouple was corrected, measures 468.0 HV1 mean.

The reasons for the escape are also confirmed. Valsangone data differed from the certificate by 40 to 44 HV on the affected lots, and incoming inspection had no step that would have shown it.

## 7. D5 and D6 Candidate corrective actions

## 1.1 Basis for the options

- Three coils of C67S strip (0.60 mm x 32 mm, 2,610 kg in total) came from Minuterie Germanasca and were received on 22 September 2026.
- The hardness specification for the strip is 430 to 470 HV1. Measured values on the three coils were 488 to 521 HV1, with a mean of 503 HV1.
- Clips formed from these coils cracked at the snap leg. The crack rate in customer assembly tests was 0.8 percent, against a normal level below 30 ppm.
- The D4 analysis found that the control thermocouple in tempering zone 3 of the supplier's furnace read about 22 °C higher than the true strip temperature. The strip was under-tempered and came out too hard.
- Two escape causes sit on our side. Incoming inspection relied on the mill certificate, and the in-house limit for finished clips was 440 to 500 HV1, which is wider than the strip specification.

The options therefore had to address three things: the supplier's furnace control, our detection at receiving, and our detection in process.

Seven candidate actions were listed in the team meeting of 9 November 2026. Each was scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (best) against five criteria. |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Option G was dropped. It would change the clip stiffness and the fit in the customer's harness clip, and the redesign effort is out of proportion to a furnace control fault. ## 1.3 Candidate corrective actions selected for trial

The team chose a layered approach. Options A, B and C form the permanent set. Options D and E are run as parallel trials because they reduce the supply and detection risk over the longer term. Option F is tested only as a way to deal with the retained stock.

The check covered 17 coils and 51 readings. The only reading outside the range on a line using the same supplier was the pair at Line 6. The coil mean was inside the range, but the supplier's tail section could be drifting. Marco Bellandi has raised this with Franco Bertola and added the coil to the weekly trend chart. Lines 4 and 6 will have their next 6 coils tested at 5 positions instead of 3.

## 2.5 Horizontal deployment to other suppliers

The same weakness, reliance on a certificate without a measurement, was found at receiving for two other strip suppliers. The plan is:

1. Extend the three-position hardness test to all hardened strip suppliers by 30 November 2026.
2. Ask each supplier for a recent furnace calibration record by 18 December 2026.
3. Add furnace thermocouple replacement intervals to the supplier questionnaire from the next audit cycle. The first audit under the new questionnaire is the one at Minuterie Germanasca on 7 December 2026.

## 2.6 Training

Twenty people will be trained between 30 November and 18 December 2026: 6 receiving staff, 14 press and packing operators. Content covers how hardness relates to crack risk in spring steel, how to read the new charts, and the reaction plan. Sofia Marchetti will organise a one-hour session for buyers on why certificates are not a substitute for measurement on safety-relevant spring parts.

## 3.6 Freight detail

There were four freight movements.

1. **Replacement strip.** One express road delivery of 900 kg of strip from the supplier on 29 September, at €1,350, so that the press could keep running while the three coils were blocked.
2. **Two premium road shipments.** Each carried 41,000 sorted clips to the customer's plant, at €1,430 each, in place of the normal weekly shipment on economy rates.
3. **One air freight pallet.** The pallet carried 38,000 clips to cover a stock-out on the customer's line, at €3,940.
4. **Customer sorting.** The customer sorted the 196,000 shipped clips on its line over 160 hours, at €32.00 per hour, as charged to us.

The extra freight cost, compared with normal economy shipments, was about 86 percent of the invoiced freight. The normal cost for the same quantities would have been about €1,490 for the sorted clip shipments, so the avoidable part of the freight is €5,310 of the €8,150 in lines 10 to 12.
